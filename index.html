Overview

Context

A leading Spanish life insurer, part of a bancassurance group

Product

Unit-linked savings plan (an investment-linked savings insurance)

Platform

Banking advisor platform used in branches, with a customer version on web and app

Status

Front end built; in development, launching in 2027

My role

Product Manager / UX lead. I owned the end-to-end user journey and wireflow, designed the research study, turned the findings into design decisions and wrote the user stories.

Cross-functional team

2 product designers, the savings squad director, the IT Product Owner, about 4 front-end and back-end developers, QA and a UX researcher

Stakeholders

Legal, Compliance, Business, Operations and the Design System team

Tools

Figma, Lovable, Rally, Confluence, Adobe Analytics

The problem

There was no online purchase flow for this product. Advisors estimated the expected return for each customer by hand.

Advisors

A slow, manual sale

A largely manual process made the conversation slow and hard to follow for the customer.

Catalogue

Too many similar products

Advisors sometimes got lost between them, and customers could not tell what a product was from its name alone.

Goal

Close the company's digital gap: a complete, omnichannel funnel from product page to checkout for both the advisor platform and the customer's own channels, that stays compliant and lets advisors close the sale in the meeting.

Research

Product discovery and research ran in two phases.

Discovery with an AI prototypeAfter a brainstorm, I built a first prototype with Lovable, before any final design existed, to react to a concrete idea in discovery sessions.
Usability testing with the design systemOnce the direction was clear, we tested prototypes built with the final design system components.
Participants

Banking advisors, end customers, and internal interviews with Legal, Compliance, Business and Operations

Methods

Recorded one-to-one interviews, AI analysis of the recordings, and a tree test to decide which features the landing page should show

How the study was designed and prioritized

I designed the research study based on stakeholders' needs, what they wanted to validate, and the business hypotheses. From there we built the interview guide and the methodology: recorded one-to-one interviews, run by the UX researcher, which I attended in part. We then used AI to analyze the recordings and find recurring patterns. How many participants shared a pattern counted, but representativeness was never the only factor in a decision.

The research report included a table of insights, each with a proposed improvement. I reviewed and approved it, identified which insights were actionable and defined the action for each one. We then prioritized the improvements by user value against effort, weighing technical effort, economic impact and the overall impact on teams' work. A workshop with the team and stakeholders then decided what made it into the flow and what didn't, and the selected improvements became prioritized user stories in the backlog.

Key insights

01

Too many similar savings products

Advisors and customers struggled to tell products apart by name.

02

A summary is not enough

Both advisors and customers valued having the full product sheet at hand before buying.

03

People need to see the outcome

We planned a linear form. Comparing portfolios and seeing a projection was key to the decision.

04

Risk has to be tangible

Customers wanted best and worst cases to understand what conservative, moderate or aggressive really means.

05

Many start with a lump sum

Customers often combine an initial one-off contribution with a monthly one, with a typical average amount.

06

Annual growth is rarely changed

Most keep the default; those who change it pick 1% or 5%, nobody goes above 10%, and many didn't understand it.

07

Questionnaires stop the sale

The MiFID test is mandatory, but customers often find it tedious, and it becomes the main stopper in the funnel. Advisors need to close the sale on the spot.

Key design decisions

01 ·

A product detail page built from what matters

Five features that matter most to the decision: tax benefits, investment options, returns, contributions and surrender. The tree test told us which to keep, and the full product sheet is always one click away. Reachable from the product catalogue and the customer record, like browse and account pages in e-commerce.

02

Validate eligibility before checkout, with actionable errors

Before asking for any data, the system checks whether the customer can buy. Each specific error (date of birth, outdated documents, non-residency, a similar product already owned, invalid account) explains what to check and links straight to the customer record.

03 ·

From a linear form to a product configurator

Contribution and portfolio are chosen together. Each portfolio card shows risk, asset mix and estimated return, and a 5-year projection with best and worst cases appears on selection. Changing the contribution resets it, so the numbers are always consistent.

04 ·

Smart defaults and suggested add-ons

The monthly contribution grows 2% a year by default to offset inflation, with only the options people use (1%, 2%, 5%, 10%) and a short definition. An optional one-off contribution suggests the average amount customers usually add.

05 ·

Compliance inside the flow, not on top of it

A 4-step checkout stepper (Details → Confirm → MiFID test → Purchase) makes the regulatory steps part of the journey, and the confirmation screen reminds that the purchase completes only once the MiFID test is done.

06 ·

Recover abandoned checkouts across channels

Share to the customer's digital banking to finish from home, or save to the advisor's platform to resume later, both within 7 days. Like a saved cart, a potential drop-off becomes a sale that can be resumed in either channel.

After purchase ·

The policy view

Key figures first (accumulated amount, return and monthly contribution), details in tabs, and every operation in one place. The page is tagged in analytics to learn which operations people really use and, with customer service queries, to decide proactive communications such as an anniversary update or how much the customer has saved, to drive engagement and retention.

Funnel

Efficiency

Time to purchase in branch

And share of purchases completed fully digitally. Are we closing the digital gap?

What I learned

We managed to turn a heavy set of legal requirements into a flow that feels simple.

Hardest trade-off

The simulator

Convincing IT to calculate projections per portfolio took real negotiation. Framing best and worst cases was legally delicate, since the worst case can mean no gain at all, and several service calls per projection made performance a risk. We settled on a fixed 5 years, the average holding period, for the MVP.

To check after launch

Three open questions

Is the simulator useful? Do people open the product sheet, when advisors move fast to close the sale? Is the MiFID test in the right place, or would it work better earlier or later?

Next

A chart for the projection

Once usage is validated: let customers pick the portfolio, then the number of years, and show the best and worst case on a chart. It's more complex and costly, so it made sense to validate the simulator first.

What I'd do differently

Workshop with Legal on day one

The legal requirements were the heaviest part of the project, and aligning on them early would have saved iterations.

Overview

Context

A leading Spanish life insurer, part of a bancassurance group

Platform

Mobile app and website

Status

Live, with ongoing iterations

Product

A self-service portal where customers who are not bank customers, and therefore have no digital banking, view and manage their savings, life insurance and pension plans

My role

Product Owner focused on UX/UI within the portal's definition team. I defined the end-to-end user journey and flows, gathered requirements with every department, turned them into user stories and owned the onboarding, the core process of the product.

Cross-functional team

I managed 2 UX designers and worked closely with a UX researcher and the portal's director, a business and commercial lead

Stakeholders

Legal, Compliance, Business, Operations, the Design System team and the identity verification provider

Tools

Figma, Maze, Rally, Confluence, Adobe Analytics

The problem

Before the portal, these customers had no digital channel. To get any information about their plan, or to withdraw from it, they had to email the insurer's customer service center. The product grew step by step:

Emailsto customer service
An online formthe MVP of the MVP
A basic websitefirst digital access
App and new websitelaunched as an MVP with view-only features
Continuous iterationself-service operations added release by release

The insurer was appointed to manage the construction sector's pension plan, the largest in Spain: close to one million participants and almost 100,000 companies. All of them needed a way in.

Onboarding was built from scratch, so the problem was not fixing a broken flow. It was designing one that met every legal and technical requirement and still felt simple: identity verification through a third-party provider, captcha, SMS verification, one-time passcode and consents.

Goal 1

Self-service

Give customers access to their products without contacting anyone.

Goal 2

Operational load

Reduce the calls, emails and forms handled by customer service.

Goal 3

Commercial consents

Grow the base that agrees to commercial communications, with the vision of adding the bank and cross-selling.

The users made it harder

Many construction workers don't have a Spanish ID card (DNI) or residence card (TIE) but a green EU registration certificate, and many people can't tell those documents apart. Each document needs a different path.

Research

Product discovery and usability testing ran in five steps.

BenchmarkFintech onboarding flows: usability trends and which data they ask for.
RequirementsGathered with every stakeholder department.
Usability testingInteractive prototype with users of each document: DNI, NIE, TIE and green certificate.
Unmoderated re-validationIn Maze, thinking aloud, after iterating.
Heuristic evaluationWith an external provider.
How findings became the backlog

Each insight came with a proposed improvement. We prioritized them by user value against effort, weighing technical effort, economic impact and the impact on teams' work, and a workshop with the team and stakeholders decided what made it into the flow. The selected improvements became prioritized user stories in the backlog.

Key insights

Overall, users found the flow intuitive and easy. The main insights:

01

Nobody knows their group

Customers had to select the company or group their plan belongs to, but most didn't know it.

02

Sign-up was hard to find

Users didn't quickly see where to create an account.

03

Password change was hard to find

Especially for existing users.

04

"Photo identification" wasn't understood

The term itself got in the way.

05

Users expected instant access

They assumed the account would be created on the spot, but it is a request that depends on an internal validation.

06

The provider felt like another product

The jump in look and feel was very noticeable, at the most sensitive moment of the flow.

07 · Heuristic findings

Use official, correct names for documents, simplify information to reduce cognitive load, unify section titles, center titles to use space better, and strengthen hierarchy and copy.

Key design decisions

Before starting: set expectations
01 ·

Set expectations before the first field

Users know upfront they need their identity document at hand, so nobody starts a process they can't finish.

02 ·

Ask for less

Every extra field is friction in the activation funnel. The first step is just first and last name, then the document type, then only what each path needs.

During sign-up: fewer, clearer choices
03 ·

One flow per document

The document decides the path: identity verification with the provider, or a manual upload. Official names, with help to tell them apart.

04 ·

Help people find their group

A predictive search ordered by volume, construction sector first, with a helper text and a lightbox if the group doesn't appear.

05

Don't duplicate existing users

Customers from the old website recover their password instead of creating a second account, then complete identity verification.

06 ·

Confirm before creating

A review screen before the one-time passcode, because an account created with wrong data is costly to fix for everyone.

After sign-up: close the loop
07 ·

A request, not an instant account

The final screen makes clear this is a sign-up request pending validation, and what happens next.

08 ·

A seamless handoff to the provider

We asked the identity verification provider to align its look and feel with our design system, so it feels like one product.

09 · Service design

Communications are part of the journey

Emails for request received, welcome, password changed and rejection with how to retry, plus retargeting for users who dropped off at identity verification.

Measuring impact

Adoption

60,000+

App installs in 18 months, tracked in Adobe Analytics, driven by onboarding and core features such as biometric login and dashboards.

Satisfaction

NPS

Post-launch tracking set up together with the CX team.

Operations

Service load

Ongoing tracking with customer service of calls and forms received, since the form is still available.

Experimentation continued after launch: many insights came after the release. Before shipping each change, we ran A/B tests gradually to decide which improvements were worth including and which weren't.

What I learned

We turned a long list of legal and technical requirements into an onboarding that users found intuitive.

Hardest trade-off

The data we ask for

It wasn't about the screens but about getting Legal, Compliance and IT to agree on what was truly required. Every field we removed had to be negotiated, and the terms and conditions alone took a long time to reach consensus.

About onboarding

It doesn't end on the last screen

The emails, the provider's screens and the retargeting are part of the same experience.

To check now that it's live

Four watch points

Does the provider handoff still feel like a break? Do users keep finding their group as more join? Is it clear this is a request, since it still causes anxiety? And technical errors, which need close monitoring.

Next

Instant, shorter, more connected

Instant account creation with feedback on the spot; a three-step sign-up without the captcha that collects the rest progressively, because we still lose people on length; and bank consents to grow a cross-selling channel.

What I'd do differently

Push for an even simpler flow from day one

Asking for consents, a captcha and photo identification all at the start costs users. I would negotiate earlier and more firmly with Legal and IT on whether the captcha and photo identification really need to come first, while respecting that they are other departments' requirements. I would also argue for creating the account directly instead of a sign-up request, because the request model still causes confusion.

The flow

OverviewThe problemResearchDesign decisionsImpactWhat I learned

Overview

Context

Self-service portal of a leading Spanish pensions, savings and life insurance provider, part of a bancassurance group

Platform

Mobile app and website

Status

Live; one improvement still in legal review

Product

Self-service management of who is paid out on a pension plan, savings plan or life insurance policy if the holder passes away

My role

Product Owner focused on UX/UI. I led the end-to-end flow, worked with Legal and Customer Experience on the designation copy, and owned the percentage-split and document-signing logic.

Cross-functional team

2 UX designers, a UX researcher and the portal's director, with close support from Customer Experience

Stakeholders

Legal, Customer Experience, Compliance, Business and the Design System team

Tools

Figma, Maze, Rally, Confluence, Adobe Analytics

The problem

There was no self-service way to change beneficiaries. Customers who wanted to update who was paid out had to email a contact form to the insurer's customer service center and wait for it to be processed manually.

We prioritized this flow for two reasons: the volume of beneficiary-related requests reaching customer service, and signals from usability testing on other parts of the app and portal, where customers repeatedly flagged it as something they wanted to manage themselves.

Changing a beneficiary is not like changing an address. It touches what happens to someone's savings after they die, so every piece of copy and every interaction needed to give the user full confidence that the change was understood and correctly applied.

The core challenge was legal rather than visual. Predefined designations had to follow the order of heirs set by Spanish inheritance law (spouse, children, parents, spouse and children), and any custom designation had to resolve into that same legal structure behind the scenes, with payout percentages split across binding levels of precedence.

Goal 1

Self-service

Let customers designate and update beneficiaries without contacting anyone.

Goal 2

Reduce operational load

Fewer emails and forms handled manually by customer service.

Goal 3

Full confidence

Make sure users understand exactly what they are changing before they confirm it.

Internally, it read like modifying an inheritance

Any communication inside this flow deals with what happens after someone's death, so it was treated with the same caution as an inheritance change — even though, legally, these products sit outside the estate.

Research

Research moved in stages, combining a manual back-office process, customer comprehension and language that Legal had shaped heavily.

Mapping the existing processI started by reviewing how beneficiary designation was being handled at the bank and on the advisor and customer platforms, then interviewed the Operations team to understand what they were still doing manually.
Three rounds of customer testingWith that groundwork, Customer Experience and I drafted the designation copy and took an early, unrefined version of the flow out to test with customers, iterating on their feedback.
A copy-focused final roundTesting kept surfacing misunderstanding of technical terms and how the copy communicated the concept — not surprising, given how much Legal had shaped the wording — so a third round focused specifically on the text, validating the revised copy with customers.

Key insights

01

“Persons named in the contract” was ambiguous

The default designation's label meant different things depending on the contract, so customers couldn't tell what it actually covered.

02

Heirs and beneficiaries got confused

Customers didn't know this product sits outside the estate, or how to correctly identify it if they wanted to designate beneficiaries through a will instead.

03

The percentage split wasn't understood

Splitting 100% of the payout across beneficiaries at each level of precedence was confusing in a custom designation.

Key design decisions

Before changing anything: build trust
01 ·

Predefined paths before custom

Spouse, children, parents and spouse and children come first, in that order, mirroring Spanish inheritance law. Most users never need to touch a percentage or a level.

02 ·

Make the legal boundary explicit

Help content clarifies that beneficiaries are not the same as heirs, that these products sit outside the estate, and exactly how to identify the product if the customer wants to designate through a will instead.

Inside personalization: the hardest trade-off
03 ·

Real-time validation to 100%

Every time a user edits one beneficiary's percentage, the rest have to be recalculated and the total re-checked — technically the most complex part of the flow — with an inline error instead of a blocking save.

04 · Legal negotiation

Simplify precedence, don't remove it

Legal would not let us drop the concept of payout levels of precedence. After many rounds of negotiation, we reduced the number of levels instead, so the complexity mostly disappears behind the predefined options.

05 ·

Cap at 6, not unlimited

Legally there is no limit, but usage data showed most customers designate one or two beneficiaries. Above 6, we ask people to contact VidaCaixa directly instead of over-building the information architecture for a residual case.

After sending: closing the loop
06 ·

Concatenate the documents

Before, users had to open each document, accept it and return to the previous screen, one by one. Now each document loads right after the last is accepted, and if the user drops off mid-way, they resume at the last document they signed.

07 · ·

A request, not an instant change

This is a request pending validation, not an instant change, so the next steps are stated upfront, and the beneficiaries section of the consultative part of the app shows the designation as “in process” until it is constituted.

08 ·

Apply the designation to other products

A usability-testing insight: customers with several products wanted one designation applied everywhere instead of repeating the process. It's in development, as it raised its own legal questions.

What I learned

We turned a legally sensitive, email-only process into a self-service flow customers understand and trust — without weakening any of the legal protections behind it.

Hardest trade-off

Negotiating precedence with Legal

It took many meetings and several rounds of negotiation to get Legal to agree to reduce the number of payout precedence levels, rather than remove the concept altogether.

About sensitive flows

Communication is part of the design

Because the change isn't instant, a clear “in process” status and follow-up emails do as much for a customer's confidence as the form itself.

What I'd do differently

Design the data model for every product, not just the first

We defined the beneficiary data fields thinking only of pension plans. When we extended the same flow to savings and life insurance, each product's services turned out to need different data: the second last name was only mandatory with a NIE or for minors with no document, and accepted document types and required fields varied by product. I'd review what every product's services need before designing shared fields, instead of adapting them after the fact.
